\title{Trading Mantissa Bits for Watts: Software Mantissa Zeroing under Module Power Caps on the NVIDIA GH200}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Koying Chen}}
% Affiliation, e-mail and acknowledgments are left for the author to fill in before submission.

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study a software knob exploiting data-dependent GPU power---zeroing unneeded low-order mantissa bits of FP64,
BF16 and FP8 operands---on 14 NVIDIA GH200 nodes under module power caps, with paired measurements and an
equivalent-power metric (the extra cap the unmodified program needs). Under a binding cap, every tested HPC program
that truncates internally and checks its results gains. At 400--800\W, tuned 8-node HPL keeping 36 significand bits
(${\approx}100\times$ residual margin) runs 4.0--11.0\pct{} faster, worth 39--127\W{} per node, and HPL-MxP keeping
3 bits 1.8--12.7\pct{} on a diagonally dominant test matrix; at 450--550\W{} Quantum ESPRESSO, with bits tied to its
Davidson tolerance, runs 2.4--4.2\pct{} faster (partly from fewer iterations) with all printed energy digits
unchanged; at 500--700\W{} DF-MP2 keeping 20--32 bits runs 4.9--8.3\pct{} faster, and a wide-range
Gaussian-process likelihood keeping 28 bits 7.4--8.2\pct, where FP32 fails and INT8 emulation is slower. At the
default 1000\W{} cap HPL is still 3.9\pct{} faster and uses 7.5\pct{} less energy per solve. Fill controls attribute
62--97\pct{} of the saving to bits that stop toggling. Safe bit counts need validation across problem sizes; the crossover with INT8 emulation of FP64 moves with matrix shape and cap. With wide per-row exponent spans,
fixed-width INT8 emulation can silently fail: at 36 bits it shifts a Gaussian-basis DFT energy by
52\,mE$_\mathrm{h}$, whereas rounding to 36 bits errs by $10^{-6}$\Eh. All-FP32 or 3xTF32 GEMMs fail on long inner
products, and in Hopper AI inference FP8 already captures most of the benefit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Power is becoming the ceiling on delivered performance: data centers cap rack power, student cluster competitions
cap whole clusters, and the NVIDIA GH200 lets users cap the entire module. Under a cap the power manager lowers the
clock until the module fits, so a program's speed depends on how much energy its operations consume.

That GPU power depends on input data is known: random operands draw far more power than constant or all-zero
ones~\cite{Bhalachandra2022,Antepara2025}, zeroed low-order bits lower GEMM power~\cite{Gregersen2024}, the gap
between all-zero and random GEMMs widens as the power limit is lowered~\cite{He2024}, and hardware can narrow or mask
mantissas~\cite{Tong2000,Pool2008,Pool2011,Guenther2016,AMD2022}. These works use special inputs such as all-zero or
constant data (He also swept the power limit, but only for all-zero against random inputs) or require new hardware or
simulated energy. To our knowledge this is the first study to truncate part of the mantissa of \emph{real} FP64/BF16
operands in software on a production GPU and measure, on multi-node benchmarks and real HPC applications checked by
their own correctness criteria, how much throughput the saved switching power buys under power caps, at what accuracy
cost, and where it still pays off against FP8 or INT8 emulation of FP64~\cite{Ozaki2025,Uchino2025}. Our contributions
are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Gains on real applications under power caps.} We express the benefit as the extra cap the unmodified
program needs to catch up, from GEMM (with paired confidence intervals) to tuned 8-node HPL (36 significand bits:
4.0--11.0\pct{} faster at 400--800\W, 39--127\W), HPL-MxP on a diagonally dominant test matrix, Quantum ESPRESSO,
power-hungry DF-MP2 (4.9--8.3\pct{} at 500--700\W) and a wide-range, power-bound Gaussian-process likelihood
(7.4--8.2\pct{} at 500--700\W), and check residuals and energies with each code's own criterion
(Sections~\ref{sec:gemm}--\ref{sec:mp2}, \ref{sec:gp}).
\item \textbf{The mechanism.} Fills of ones or a fixed pattern instead of zeros separate ``no toggling'' from ``value
is zero'' in tensor-core GEMM, FP64 FMA units and memory traffic, quantifying the toggling hypothesis on GH200 module
power (Section~\ref{sec:why}).
\item \textbf{Bit counts and failure boundaries.} A rule using only the solver's own tolerance leaves every printed
digit of the Quantum ESPRESSO energies unchanged; we test how far HPL (several sizes), PySCF, third-party non-GEMM
codes, LoRA fine-tuning and six language models can be truncated, and the role of rounding
(Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:ai}).
\item \textbf{The boundary with format changes.} Against INT8 emulation (cuBLAS, GEMMul8) and FP32/3xTF32 for FP64,
and FP8 for AI, the crossover moves with shape, power cap, accumulation precision and exponent span; with wide
per-row exponent spans, fixed-width INT8 emulation can silently err, a risk that cheap per-row span measurements
flag beforehand (Sections~\ref{sec:format}--\ref{sec:ai}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Input-dependent GPU power.} Bhalachandra et al.\ measured 67\pct{} more DGEMM power on random than on constant
operands on A100~\cite{Bhalachandra2022}. Gregersen et al.\ varied CUTLASS GEMM inputs on V100, A100 and H100, saw
power differ by up to almost 40\pct{} with zeroed low bits lowering it, and proposed that toggling drives it, but
studied only GEMM at sizes that do not throttle~\cite{Gregersen2024}. He found all-zero $8192^3$ GEMMs on A100 faster
than random ones, a speedup that grew from about 1.2$\times$ to about 2.3$\times$ as the power limit fell from 330 to
150\W, before falling at 100\W~\cite{He2024}. On GH200, zero-input DGEMM draws 563\W{} against 898\W{} for random
input~\cite{Antepara2025}, while vector FP64 performance shows no significant difference~\cite{Antepara2025W}.
Constant and random data can differ by about 100\W{} on H100/H200~\cite{Ujeniya2026}, and a GTX\,580 ALU power model
is dominated by toggles, with the number of ones secondary~\cite{Lucas2016}. None of these truncates part of the
mantissa of real data or evaluates accuracy or applications.

\textbf{Power-constrained HPC.} Under a power bound, manufacturing variation makes nominally identical processors
differ by about 6\pct{} in execution time~\cite{Rountree2012} and per-rank DGEMM performance by up to 64\pct, which
variability-aware power allocation mitigates~\cite{Inadomi2015}; overprovisioning adds nodes under a fixed system
budget~\cite{Patki2013}, and Zeus co-tunes GPU power limit and batch size for DNN training energy~\cite{Zeus2023}.
These works distribute power; we lower the energy per operation, so the two compose. GH200 CPU and GPU share module
power, and the GPU clock drops near the cap~\cite{Patrou2025}; nvidia-smi samples only about 25\pct{} of runtime on
A100/H100~\cite{Yang2024}, which our long steady windows and pairing mitigate.

\textbf{Hardware masking and data-movement energy.} Narrower floating-point multipliers save up to 66\pct{} energy
per operation~\cite{Tong2000}, and precision has been reduced or masked in mobile-GPU shaders~\cite{Pool2008,Pool2011},
graphics register files~\cite{Angerd2017}, a baseband processor~\cite{Guenther2016}, GPU shaders~\cite{AMD2022} and,
in simulation, tensor-core register files~\cite{Jahadi2024}; rpe and chop emulate low precision with the same bit
operations without measuring power~\cite{Dawson2017,Higham2019}. DRAM energy depends on toggling
rates~\cite{Chatterjee2017,OConnor2017,Ghose2018}, all-zero inputs draw less power as fewer DRAM and L2 bits
toggle~\cite{TensorRTDocs}, and compression can increase toggles~\cite{Pekhimenko2016}. We change neither hardware
nor data format.

\textbf{Precision tuning and mixed precision.} Precision tuning~\cite{RubioGonzalez2013}, mixed-precision
storage~\cite{Higham2022} and transprecision computing~\cite{Malossi2018} change types or bytes; zeroing changes
neither and composes with them. Reading only leading mantissa segments speeds up memory-bound PageRank by 10--30\pct{}
by saving bandwidth~\cite{Grutzmacher2020}. Low-precision LU with iterative refinement~\cite{Haidar2018} underlies
HPL-MxP~\cite{Dongarra2025}. The information content of mantissa bits, estimated from the data~\cite{Klower2021},
could predict how many bits GEMM operands need.

\textbf{Emulating FP64 on low-precision units.} The Ozaki scheme~\cite{Ozaki2012} splits a matrix product into
exactly computed products of lower-precision slices; it runs on integer units~\cite{Ootomo2024}, and Ozaki
scheme~II~\cite{Ozaki2025} uses modular arithmetic in GEMMul8, whose speed and energy efficiency Uchino et al.\
evaluated~\cite{Uchino2025}. cuBLAS's Ozaki-type emulation scales each row of $A$ and column of $B$ by a shared factor,
so a row's range is encoded in the mantissa; its automatic mode picks the slice count from the exponent span and falls
back to native FP64 beyond a library-defined maximum~\cite{cuBLASEmu,Schwarz2026}. Zeroing keeps the original units,
so the two can be complementary; we compare them at equal cap and comparable accuracy.

\textbf{Mixed precision in geostatistics.} ExaGeoStat evaluates Gaussian-process likelihoods with an FP64 tile
Cholesky~\cite{Abdulah2018}; its successors keep diagonal tiles in FP64 and convert others, chosen by distance or
norm, to FP32, FP16 or FP8 (1.6$\times$ on average with FP32~\cite{Abdulah2019}, up to 2.64$\times$ adding
FP16~\cite{Abdulah2022}, 12$\times$ with tile low-rank compression~\cite{Cao2022}, 3$\times$ on a
GH200~\cite{Ren2025}), also in a climate emulator~\cite{Abdulah2024}. Cao et al.\ measured power and energy of such
factorizations on single GPUs, without caps or a clock analysis~\cite{Cao2023}. Uniform single precision is
insufficient here~\cite{Abdulah2022}, as we also find; truncation composes with tile conversion
(Section~\ref{sec:gp}).

\textbf{Convergence-driven precision.} LOBPCG switched between FP32, FP64 and mixed precision by convergence state
runs up to 8.5$\times$ faster on GPUs~\cite{Woo2023}; GPU4PySCF can set its number of INT8 emulation slices from the
SCF energy change, keeping errors below $10^{-7}$\,hartree (E$_\mathrm{h}$; 1\,E$_\mathrm{h}$ = 2\,Ry =
627.5\,kcal/mol)~\cite{Huang2026}; RI-MP2 has run with single- and mixed-precision GEMMs~\cite{OlivaresAmaya2010}.
These gain speed from a faster format or unit and do not study power; we stay on FP64 units with bit counts set by the
Davidson threshold and gain mainly from clock headroom under a cap (on one system also from fewer Davidson iterations;
Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).

\section{Why Fewer Mantissa Bits Save Power}\label{sec:why}

CMOS dynamic power is roughly $\alpha_\mathrm{sw} C V^2 f$ with data-dependent switching activity
$\alpha_\mathrm{sw}$. Under a cap the power manager lowers $f$ (and $V$) until the module fits, so a lower
$\alpha_\mathrm{sw}$ yields higher clock and throughput, and below the cap lower power. GH200 shows both: at the
default 1000\W{} cap the native FP64 GEMM is still capped 95\pct{} of the time at 1822\,MHz, while keeping 0 bits it
runs at the maximum 1980\,MHz and module power falls from 984 to 707\W; under a 700\W{} cap the SM clock is
1502\,MHz native, 1668\,MHz with 27 kept bits and 1965\,MHz with 0, all at about 694\W.

\textbf{Toggling or zero?} Zeroing both stops the low bits from varying between elements and makes them zero. Fills
of all ones or of a fixed nonzero pattern shared by all elements separate the two: neither toggles, neither is zero.
At 1000\W{} all fills run at 1980\,MHz, so we compare module power (as the cap holds the native GEMM at 984\W, the
share attributed to ``no toggling'' is conservative); at 500\W{} every configuration is always capped, so we compare
throughput (one node, two runs each; Table~\ref{tab:fill}). Merely stopping the toggling captures 68--92\pct{} of the
benefit in tensor-core GEMM, quantifying the toggling hypothesis~\cite{Gregersen2024} on GH200 module power; zeros
save somewhat more. With 20 kept bits this fits multiplier structure: low bits of $a$ fixed at one still form partial
products $a_i b_j$ that toggle with the kept bits of $b$, while zeros keep that slice at zero. With 0 kept bits no
partial product toggles under any fill, yet zeros still save more than ones (707 versus 774\W), perhaps because exact
powers of two align and accumulate more simply (unverified). Lacking gate-level data, both are
inferences, consistent with a GTX\,580 ALU model in which toggles dominate and the number of ones is
secondary~\cite{Lucas2016}.

\textbf{Not just tensor cores.} Keeping 0 bits lowers module power at 1000\W{} from 862 to 553\W{} in an FP64
CUDA-core multiply-add loop and from 672 to 549\W{} ($-18\pct$) in a STREAM-like copy at 3.33\,TB/s, and ones or the
fixed pattern keep 62--87\pct{} and 86--97\pct{} of these savings (Table~\ref{tab:fill}): fewer toggles in arithmetic
units and on data wires are the main cause, also for bandwidth-bound code, consistent with toggle-dependent DRAM
energy~\cite{Chatterjee2017,Ghose2018}. An Altera patent's qualitative claim that zeroing low bits at an FPGA
floating-point multiplier's input barely reduces power, as the compression tree keeps switching~\cite{Altera2017},
does not contradict this: on a GPU the bits also travel through registers, buses and memory, and even GH200
multipliers save more with zeros than with ones.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\textbf{Mantissa zeroing.} For an operand with $p$ fraction bits (52 for FP64, 7 for BF16, 3 for FP8 E4M3) we keep the
top $k$ and zero the rest; sign and exponent are untouched. Truncation toward zero is one bit mask; round-to-nearest
first adds half a unit of the last kept bit, stochastic rounding a uniform random integer below the weight of the
discarded bits. Ties go away from zero in the shim, GEMM microbenchmark, Quantum ESPRESSO, DF-MP2 and Gaussian-process
code, and to even in HPL, LoRA weight loading and LLM inference. ``Truncate to $k$ bits'' covers both: unless stated
otherwise, the applications (Quantum ESPRESSO, PySCF, DF-MP2, Gaussian process, ExaGeoStat, third-party codes) and HPL
round to nearest, and the GEMM microbenchmarks, crossover sweeps and HPL-MxP truncate toward zero. HPL counts
significand bits including the hidden bit, as rocHPL does (36 bits are 35 fraction bits).

\textbf{Implementation and cost.} Each element costs one integer AND (plus an add for rounding), fusable into the
kernel that produces the operand, as in our microbenchmarks and HPL-MxP; HPL rounds both operands in a separate kernel
before each DGEMM; Quantum ESPRESSO (Davidson vectors), DF-MP2 and the Gaussian-process code zero operands in place;
LoRA zeroes the frozen FP8 base weights once after loading. Unmodified software runs under an \texttt{LD\_PRELOAD} shim
that intercepts cuBLAS and CPU GEMM calls, writes zeroed copies of $A$ and $B$ to scratch buffers and can record
exponent histograms and per-row spans. Measured times include HPL's extra
kernel and the shim's copies.

\textbf{Error model.} For operands uniform on $[-1,1)$ with $A$ and $B$ zeroed,
$\lVert \hat C - C\rVert_F/\lVert C\rVert_F \approx \alpha\,2^{-k}$ for $C=AB$, with $\alpha\approx0.27$ (nearest),
0.38 (stochastic) and 0.70 (truncation) for $k\ge4$, independent of the inner dimension from 256 to 16384.
Truncation's error is almost entirely a systematic shrinkage (least-squares slope of $\hat C$ against $C$ about
$1-0.6\cdot 2^{-k}$), which matters little for solvers that iterate to self-consistency or correct with a residual but
hurts where bias accumulates. On real data the $2^{-k}$ law holds within each GEMM, but $\alpha$ grows with
cancellation (nearest: 0.19--0.32 on four Quantum ESPRESSO Si\,1000 GEMMs, 0.07 to 9.6 on four gold-surface GEMMs),
so it must be measured per application; self-consistent iteration corrects these errors, which is why Quantum
ESPRESSO tolerates 20--28 bits even with $\alpha\approx 10$.

\textbf{Equivalent power saving.} If the zeroed program reaches performance $p'(P)$ under cap $P$, its equivalent
power saving $\Delta W(P) = P_\mathrm{native}\bigl(p'(P)\bigr) - P$ is the extra per-node power the native program
needs to match, with $P_\mathrm{native}(x)$, the cap at which native reaches $x$, interpolated linearly along the
measured performance--cap curve. If $p'$ exceeds every native point we report a lower bound; if the GPU is not
power-bound, the saving appears as lower power at equal speed. As performance is concave in the cap, interpolating
between widely spaced caps (e.g., 800 and 1000\W) slightly overestimates $\Delta W$.

\textbf{Tolerance-driven precision.} In plane-wave DFT each Davidson diagonalization gets a threshold
$\mathit{ethr}$ that tightens from about $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-12}$ during the self-consistent-field (SCF) iteration. We
keep $k = \mathrm{clamp}(\lceil -\log_2 \mathit{ethr}\rceil + m,\ k_{\min},\ 52)$ fraction bits (margin $m$, floor
$k_{\min}=16$). This empirical rule is not a guarantee: $\mathit{ethr}$ is an absolute eigenvalue tolerance (in Ry),
not a relative mantissa error, and the margin absorbs the difference; it needs only a tolerance the application
already computes.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{System.} Fourteen nodes, each with one GH200 480GB superchip (72-core Grace; Hopper GPU with 96\,GB HBM3,
maximum SM clock 1980\,MHz, FP64 tensor-core peak 67\,TFLOP/s~\cite{NVIDIAGH200}), 200\,Gb/s InfiniBand, NVHPC 26.5
(CUDA 13.2, cuBLAS 13.4). The module power cap covers GPU, CPU and memory (the GPU alone also has a 900\W{} limit);
the driver accepts 200--1000\W, the datasheet lists 450--1000\W~\cite{NVIDIAGH200}, so our caps below 450\W{} lie
outside it (measured power still follows them). The default 1000\W{} is the highest cap, not an uncapped state: native
HPL and FP64 GEMM still hit it often.

\textbf{Workloads.} cuBLAS GEMMs on operands uniform on $[-1,1)$ (BF16 and FP8 E4M3 at
$32768{\times}32768{\times}6144$, FP32 accumulation); HPL (CUDA
port of rocHPL) and HPL-MxP (BF16 LU, FP32 accumulation, FP64 refinement) on 8 nodes; Quantum ESPRESSO 7.6
GPU~\cite{Giannozzi2020} (Si 1000-atom supercell, DGEMM; Au 112-atom surface with $k$ points, ZGEMM);
GPU4PySCF 1.8.1~\cite{Wu2025,Li2025} (valinomycin B3LYP/def2-TZVP with density fitting; taxol RHF and DF-MP2); MFEM,
SW4, LAMMPS; a Gaussian-process likelihood; MLPerf Training Llama\,2 70B LoRA (NVIDIA's v5.0 FP8 code, 8
nodes, global batch 8); and LLM inference (six models).

\textbf{Measurement protocol.} nvidia-smi samples power, SM clock, temperature and throttle reasons on every node
every 250--500\,ms. Each measurement starts below 45\,\textdegree C, and within a cap the variants run in ABBA order
(native, zeroed, zeroed, native) on the same nodes. Windows whose recorded cap differs from the intended one are
discarded (another user's tool reset the cluster's caps at least twice; affected measurements were rerun).
Power-brake counters were enabled only in a diagnostic job, not in the main HPL and HPL-MxP sweeps. The first native
run of a job is often slower (warm-up), so, as ABBA sequences usually start with native, the first pair can overstate
the gain (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).

\textbf{Power, energy and statistics.} Energy integrates the driver's 1-s averaged module power, summed over nodes.
Reported power is the mean of instantaneous samples in a steady window, within $\pm0.6\pct$ of the 1-s average over
845 windows (energy avoids single readings, which deviate by $-37$ to $+41\pct$ on bursty kernels). While
capped, the 1-s average often lies below the cap, so the limiter acts on a shorter, unknown time scale. Under GEMM load
the GPU draws 88--90\pct{} of module power at 900--1000\W{} and 77\pct{} at 400\W. Intervals are 95\pct{} $t$
intervals of paired differences (same node, cap and ABBA round), removing the 13--17\pct{} node-to-node clock spread;
only GEMM (3 nodes $\times$ 2 pairs, not fully independent, so possibly narrow) and the Quantum ESPRESSO adaptive runs
(6 pairs) have them, while HPL, HPL-MxP, DF-MP2 and Gaussian-process cells have two rounds on one node set.

The HPL residual check determines the usable bit count, and it must be checked at several sizes
(Table~\ref{tab:hpl}). From 36 to 28 bits the residual grows 86--410$\times$, of the order of $2^8=256$, so 28 bits
sits less than one bit from the threshold and is not an operating point, whereas 36 bits leaves a margin of about
100$\times$ (at least 98$\times$, about seven bits). We first tuned native HPL at 1000\W. The configuration inherited from an H200 study ($N=262144$, NB 1024,
$2\times4$, default NCCL) gave 374.8\,TFLOP/s; fixing NCCL at 8 channels with 2\,MiB buffers (+4.5\pct) and raising
$N$ to 286720 (+2\pct) gave 390.2--398.8\,TFLOP/s in three runs, and every neighboring setting of $N$, NB, split
fraction and NCCL parameters fell within this ${\approx}2\pct$ run-to-run spread (26 settings; $4\times2$ grids were
14\pct{} slower). With 36 bits at the tuned configuration, HPL is 4.0--11.0\pct{} faster at 400--800\W{}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hpl}), $\Delta W=39$--127\W{} (the 127\W{} at 800\W{} interpolates across the 800--1000\W{} gap and
overestimates), and uses 4.1--9.3\pct{} less energy. At the default 1000\W{} cap native HPL is still capped in
75\pct{} of the samples (SM 1936\,MHz) against 7\pct{} for the 36-bit run, which is therefore 3.9\pct{} faster and
uses 7.5\pct{} less energy per solve. The untuned configuration gave 3.4--8.3\pct{} at 400--800\W{} and
0.7/7.7\pct{} at 1000\W. (28 bits is 3.8--16.3\pct{} faster, but its residual reaches 15.4 at $N=286720$; we list
it only as a boundary reference.)

HPL-MxP (BF16 LU with FP64 refinement) zeroes the BF16 operands whenever they are written back. Our runs use a test

only''~\cite{HPLMxPRules}, and on such a matrix even coarse $L$ and $U$ factors precondition well, so the stability
reported here may be a property of this matrix and cannot be assumed for the generators allowed in official runs. We
use 3 kept bits (Table~\ref{tab:hplperf} lists the delivered rates of both benchmarks), stable at all four sizes
$N=245760$--491520 (residual 1.4--3.8, always 3 refinement iterations). At $N=491520$ this is

HPL-MxP at 1000\W{} trips the chassis hardware power brake (4--10\,s each time; in the diagnostic job with counters
enabled, the 0-bit version did not), so we exclude 1000\W{} from speedups.

\subsection{Plane-Wave DFT and Quantum Chemistry}\label{sec:sci}

Plane-wave DFT is tolerant: with 28 kept bits the total energies of Quantum ESPRESSO Si\,1000 and Au\,112 move by
$\ee{1.0}{-5}$ and $\ee{2.3}{-5}$\Ry{} under truncation and by $\ee{3.5}{-7}$ and $\ee{2.8}{-7}$\Ry{} under
round-to-nearest. With nearest rounding the gold surface still converges at 20 bits ($\ee{5.5}{-5}$\Ry, both runs),
while for Si\,1000 20 bits is the failure edge (one run converges with $\ee{1.1}{-4}$\Ry, the other aborts at
iteration 24); truncation diverges or slows at 20 bits. Gaussian-basis chemistry needs more: GPU4PySCF valinomycin DFT
errs by $\ee{1.1}{-8}$\Eh{} at 44 bits; at 36 bits the energy oscillates near $10^{-8}$\Eh{} and misses a $10^{-10}$
threshold, but with the chemically sufficient $10^{-7}$ threshold 40, 36 and 32 bits converge in the native 12
iterations (32-bit nearest error $\ee{1.2}{-5}$\Eh, $\approx$0.007\,kcal/mol), consistent with the $\approx$37
significant bits that suffice for density-matrix purification~\cite{Dawson2024}.

Through the shim (every GEMM truncated; the baseline takes the same copy path), Si\,1000 at 550\W{} with 28-bit
nearest rounding is 6.2\pct{} faster with 6.4\pct{} less energy and Au\,112 at 450\W{} 4.9\pct{} faster with 5.1\pct{}
less energy (one run each, between baseline runs). These are upper bounds for in-library truncation; against the
unmodified program the shim's $\approx$5\pct{} copy cost makes it 0.4\pct{} slower (Au\,112, 450\W).

\section{When Format Changes Win and When They Fail}\label{sec:format}

Truncation keeps every element's exponent, whereas per-tensor scaled formats (FP32, FP8) need the whole tensor
inside their range and Ozaki-type emulation slices each row (column) relative to its maximum, losing elements more
than $2^{-k}$ below it for $k$ kept bits. Operands span 12--14 binades (central 99.9\pct) in HPL, about 13 in
Llama\,2 70B weights, 71--93 in Quantum ESPRESSO and 371--690 in valinomycin (down to $2^{-691}$, beyond FP32's
range): plane-wave DFT (too wide for FP16 or FP8, modest precision) has no smaller-format alternative to zeroing,
Gaussian-basis chemistry needs both range and precision, and LLMs and HPL can move to smaller formats.

\subsection{Crossover with INT8 Emulation of FP64}\label{sec:crossover}

error on the same node and cap ($32768{\times}32768{\times}2048$, 400--1000\W, ABBA), three slices ($\ee{5}{-7}$) beat

\subsection{FP32 and 3xTF32: Accumulation, Not Range}\label{sec:fp32}

3xTF32 (our own implementation in the shim and a benchmark script, not CUTLASS's) is the fastest method in
Table~\ref{tab:methods} but only FP32-accurate: its error grows almost linearly with the inner dimension
(7.5--7.9$\times$ per 8$\times$ in $k$; $\ee{4.8}{-6}$ at $k=2048$ on $4096{\times}4096$, 18 and 7 times that of FP64
rounded to nearest and truncated to 20 bits), whereas FP32 SGEMM grows as $\sqrt{k}$ and FP64 truncation not at all.
This points to systematic error in TF32 tensor-core accumulation, consistent with the round-toward-zero accumulation
found on V100, T4 and A100~\cite{Fasi2021} (bit-accurate Hopper models:~\cite{Khattak2025}).

Quantum ESPRESSO's operands fit in FP32's range, so we replaced every FP64 and complex FP64 GEMM with single precision
through the shim (3xTF32 multiplies big and small FP32 parts three times on TF32 tensor cores; $C$ is combined in
FP64). On the gold surface at 450\W{} (two interleaved runs each) FP32 aborts at SCF iteration 26, and 3xTF32 and TF32
at iteration 2, as the Davidson bands fail to converge and the integrated charge drifts, while FP64 rounded to 24 and
20 bits stays within $\ee{8.1}{-6}$ and $\ee{5.5}{-5}$\Ry{} of native in 28 iterations; on Si\,1000 at 550\W{} FP32
and 3xTF32 abort at iterations 14 and 9. The cause is accumulation over the plane waves (inner dimension 95386 on the
gold surface plus a real GEMM with $k=4.3{\times}10^{6}$; 375286 for Si\,1000), where 3xTF32 already errs by
$\ee{5.6}{-5}$ to $\ee{2.2}{-4}$, the same order as plain TF32. FP32 GEMM on Hopper is also slower than FP64 tensor
cores (an estimated 1.5$\times$ per SCF iteration on the gold surface). Replacing every GEMM with single precision
thus fails for this long-inner-product solver although the range fits; FP32 in selected steps only~\cite{Woo2023} is a
different matter.

\textbf{Wide range, partly power-bound DFT.} These DFT runs draw only 650--690\W{} at 1000\W{} but are power-bound
below. For valinomycin (grid level 6) at 550 and 450\W, rounding to 36 bits through the shim is 3.3--3.5\pct{} faster
than the copy baseline, with 2--3\pct{} less energy and an error of $\ee{9.5}{-7}$\Eh, while correct emulation with
40 bits, about ten times more accurate ($\ee{8.4}{-8}$\Eh), takes 1.48--1.53$\times$ the baseline time (two rounds
each). On the default grid, where FP64 GEMM takes 32\pct{} of the runtime, we estimate only
$32\pct\times5$--$6.5\pct\approx1.6$--2.1\pct{} at 500--800\W{} (GEMM speedup at 35 bits; GEMM time assumed
proportional to flops at 1000\W{} speed). Neither number means truncation beats INT8 emulation here: choosing by shape
(Table~\ref{tab:shape}) bounds the gain at about 3.5\pct{} with cuBLAS emulation for the long-$k$ shape, or 6.6\pct{}
with the less accurate GEMMul8.

\subsection{Wide Range and Power-Bound: Gaussian-Process Geostatistics}\label{sec:gp}

Gaussian-process maximum-likelihood estimation factors an $n{\times}n$ Matérn covariance matrix by Cholesky at every
likelihood evaluation; as in HPL almost all work is FP64 GEMM, but the covariance decays exponentially with distance.
Our ExaGeoStat-like kernel uses $n=80000$ random 2-D locations, Matérn-3/2 covariance (nugget $10^{-4}$), blocked
Cholesky ($n_b=1024$, cuBLAS DGEMM trailing updates) and six range parameters (0.02--0.08); each panel $L_{21}$ keeps
$k$ bits in place before the trailing update in one fused pass. Native draws 953--970\W{} at 1000\W{} and is capped
91--94\pct{} of the time; the sampled panel rows span 162 binades, and 88\pct{} of panel nonzeros lie over $2^{-32}$
below their row maximum.

The log-likelihood is about 1.2--1.7$\times10^5$, so the 28-bit deviation of $\ee{7.2}{-4}$ is far below what matters
(Table~\ref{tab:gp}). At 1000\W, where native is capped 91--94\pct{} of the time, 28 bits is
3.0--3.9\pct{} faster (36 bits 2.4--3.2\pct), but this depends on the setting: with the Morton ordering used below
($n=60000$; native capped in only 19--54\pct{} of the samples) 28 bits gains +0.0/+0.1\pct, and the fit below
1.8--2.3\pct. FP32's deviation makes it unusable, possibly because the covariance's conditioning worsens with $n$ (at
$n=8000$ FP32 still finds the same estimate). cuBLAS emulation is less accurate than 28-bit truncation and slower,
partly because the trailing update's inner dimension equals the block size, 1024, a shape that favors truncation
(Section~\ref{sec:crossover}); despite a wider per-row span than in the DFT case it only loses accuracy instead of
going wrong, so a wide span is necessary but not sufficient for failure, which
also depends on what the dropped small elements contribute.

\textbf{A one-parameter maximum-likelihood fit.} We fitted the range parameter, with variance and smoothness
fixed ($n=60000$; 1000 and 700\W; two interleaved rounds).
Truncation to 36 and 28 bits and cuBLAS emulation return the native $\hat\theta=0.05007952$ in every printed digit,
with maximized log-likelihoods within $\ee{4.8}{-6}$ (36 bits) and $\ee{2.3}{-4}$ (28 bits) of native, while FP32
moves $\hat\theta$ by 0.49\pct{} and loses 0.72. With 28 and 36 bits the fit is 7.1--7.3\pct{} and 4.8--4.9\pct{}
faster at 700\W{} (6.6--6.8\pct{} and 4.5--4.8\pct{} less energy) and 1.8--2.3\pct{} and 1.6--2.2\pct{} faster at
1000\W{} (9.1--9.9\pct{} and 3.5--5.6\pct{} less energy); emulation is 32--41\pct{} slower. Of the three whole-matrix
choices tested (FP32, 36-bit emulation, truncation), only truncation is both fast and accurate here.

\textbf{Composing with tile mixed precision.} With the locations in Morton order, we let the trailing update use FP16
inputs with FP32 accumulation on the tensor cores for tiles below each column's FP64 extent, chosen by the tile-norm
rule for a required accuracy of $10^{-9}$ as in~\cite{Cao2023}. Over the six ranges ($n=60000$, two ABBA rounds) this
is 1.60--1.63$\times$ faster than native FP64 at 1000, 700 and 500\W, with log-likelihood deviations up to 0.043.
Truncating its remaining FP64 tiles to 28 bits adds 1.9\pct{} at 1000\W{} and 7.0\pct{} and 6.1\pct{} at 700 and
500\W{} with an unchanged deviation, so truncation composes with format changes rather than competing with them.

\textbf{ExaGeoStat itself.} Through the shim, the MLE of ExaGeoStatCPP (cuBLAS~13.6; $N=60000$) factorizes
3.0/4.0\pct{} faster with 36/28 bits than a same-copy baseline at 1000\W, where its GPU part is already partly
power-limited, 5.0/8.0\pct{} at 600\W{} and 5.6/9.4\pct{} at 450\W{} (means of two ABBA rounds, which differ widely at
450\W, +6.9 and +11.9\pct{} with 28 bits, as the CPU shares the module cap), with
log-likelihoods within $\ee{9.8}{-8}$ relative. The shim's 7--9\pct{} copy cost leaves it 0.5--3.1\pct{} slower than
unmodified ExaGeoStat, so the gain needs an in-library implementation; its \texttt{-{}-computation=mixed} option is not
implemented, so we could not compare with its own mixed precision.

\section{AI Workloads: Training and Inference}\label{sec:ai}

\textbf{Training: truncating FP8 base weights is a negative result.} MLPerf Training measures time to target quality
(eval loss $\le0.925$ on GovReport for Llama\,2 70B LoRA). Truncating only the frozen FP8 base weights to 2 bits after
loading (the rest follows the FP8 recipe~\cite{Micikevicius2022}) made each 384 samples 0.9--1.2\pct{} faster but
raised the samples to target from 3456 to 4224 with the same seed (22\pct{} slower at 575\W, 19\pct{} at 800\W; a
second seed at 800\W: 3072 to 4224, +39\pct); 1 bit did not converge. The first evaluation is always 0.008--0.010
higher (two seeds, three pairs), but native convergence differs by one evaluation interval between node sets (3072
versus 3456), so we read this only as evidence that FP8's 3 mantissa bits leave no room; rounding modes, decisive
in~\cite{Gupta2015}, show no consistent order.

\textbf{Inference: FP8 has already taken most of the benefit.} BF16 rounded to nearest at 3 bits has FP8 E4M3's
mantissa width and accuracy (GEMM error on uniform data: 3.34\pct{} versus 3.6--3.9\pct; truncation needs 4 bits): on
six language models (6.7B--70B) WikiText-2 perplexity rises by 0.41--2.00\pct{} versus 0.45--2.23\pct{} (per-tensor)
and 0.32--0.88\pct{} (per-channel) for FP8, and the two tie where FP8 is known to fail~\cite{Zhang2025} (attention
perplexity at 2k/8k context: +0.49/+1.06\pct{} versus +0.52/+1.08\pct{} for emulated FP8; 8k--32k key retrieval: both
100\pct). Yet FP8 GEMMs are about 1.7--1.9$\times$ as fast (575\W: 1.67--1.72$\times$ BF16 with 3 bits,
1.80--1.87$\times$ native BF16). Without switching to 2:4 structured sparsity or INT4 weights, zeroing on top of FP8
is the remaining way to beat FP8 on Hopper, which lacks FP4, but it trades accuracy for speed about one for one:
keeping 2 bits of real Llama2-70B layer-40 activations and weights is 4.4/3.2/5.0/8.2\pct{} faster at
1000/700/575/450\W{} (weights only: 1.3--3.3\pct) while 70B perplexity rises by 3.4\pct. Bandwidth-bound decode gains
little: at 1000\W{} zeroing leaves its speed unchanged on uniform data (2.6--3.0\pct{} faster at the same clock on
real weights), and truncating weights to 2 or 0 bits saves only 1.5\pct{} or 5.5\pct{} of power at an accuracy cost.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Benefit under a fixed power budget.} Under a fixed power budget, such as a rack limit, the equivalent power
saving becomes usable compute. At 8 nodes $\times$ 700\W, tuned HPL with the safe 36 bits rises from 346.2 to
362.9\,TFLOP/s (+4.8\pct); native HPL would need about 768\W{} per node, about 0.5\,kW more for the cluster, to match.
At the default 1000\W{} cap it still gains 3.9\pct{} in speed and 7.5\pct{} in energy per solve. The
cost is one bitwise operation per element, fusable into the producing kernel, and no hardware.

\textbf{Benchmark rules.} TOP500 Linpack requires full 64-bit floating-point arithmetic and excludes lower-precision
solves with iterative refinement and integer emulation of floating point~\cite{TOP500Linpack}; HPL on truncated
operands misses the full-precision requirement too. Since June 2025 HPL-MxP requires at least 16-bit floating point
and allows strictly diagonally dominant matrices for testing only~\cite{HPLMxPRules}; BF16 keeping 3 bits is
effectively 12-bit, and our test matrix is not allowed for official runs. All HPL and HPL-MxP numbers here are thus
research results, not official scores. MLPerf Inference's closed division allows pure mathematical quantization but
forbids pruning non-zero weights~\cite{MLPerfInfRules}, and zeroing is the former; whether MLPerf Training permits
truncated weights depends on each round's rules.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~One GH200 cluster (an earlier report has only preliminary H200 and MI350X results). (ii)~The
HPL residual is a deterministic but chaotic function of $N$ (28 bits passes at $N=131072$, 262144 and 286720, all close to
the threshold, and fails at 196608), so operating points (36 bits for HPL, 3 for HPL-MxP) were validated at several sizes. (iii)~HPL-MxP's
strictly diagonally dominant test matrix with approximate diagonal factorization is allowed only for
testing~\cite{HPLMxPRules}; its stability at 3 kept bits may be a property of this matrix. (iv)~Each scientific code
ran one or two inputs, without forces, geometry optimization or larger systems. (v)~Shim copies cost 3--9\pct{} (about
5\pct{} for Quantum ESPRESSO, 7--9\pct{} for ExaGeoStat), so shim results use a same-copy baseline. (vi)~Nodes differ
by 13--17\pct{} at equal power, so comparisons are paired on the same nodes (ABBA or seed); only GEMM and Quantum
ESPRESSO have intervals, other cells two rounds on one node set, and the crossover sweep ran its four $n$ on different
nodes. (vii)~The default 1000\W{} cap is the highest, not uncapped; the limiter's time scale is unknown; caps below
450\W{} lie outside the datasheet range~\cite{NVIDIAGH200}. (viii)~Cooling and the chassis supply change conclusions
(at low fan speed the GPU hits its thermal limit first; at full fan speed native HPL-MxP at 1000\W{} trips the power
brake) and must be reported. (ix)~Another user's tool reset the shared cluster's caps at least twice; every window
checks the cap in effect. (x)~Power comes from the driver's sensor, which on A100/H100 samples only part of the
time~\cite{Yang2024}; our paired relative comparisons use one path and steady 10--20\,s windows, but absolute energy
needs an external meter. (xi)~Fill controls cover only 0 and 20 kept bits, the 500\W{} rows one node and two runs, and
the extra saving of zeros at 0 kept bits is unexplained. (xii)~About half of the gold-surface Quantum ESPRESSO gain
comes from fewer Davidson iterations, not power, for unidentified reasons. (xiii)~Real-data emulation comparisons on
Quantum ESPRESSO ran once per setting; GEMMul8 ran out of GPU memory on two applications. (xiv)~The Gaussian-process
results come from our ExaGeoStat-like kernel on synthetic data with a simplified mixed-precision re-implementation
(FP64 storage, one FP16 GEMM per panel over the tiles that need it); ExaGeoStat itself was measured only through the
shim, whose copy cost offsets the gain.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On a production GH200 cluster, when the GPU is
power-bound and truncation is done inside the program, the HPC programs we tested that check their own results all
gain: at 400--800\W, tuned HPL keeping 36 significand bits is 4.0--11.0\pct{} faster (39--127\W{} equivalent) and HPL-MxP
keeping 3 bits 1.8--12.7\pct{} on a diagonally dominant test matrix; Quantum ESPRESSO with tolerance-driven bit counts
is 2.4--4.2\pct{} faster at 450--550\W{} with every printed digit of the energies unchanged (on the gold surface about
half of the gain comes from fewer Davidson iterations); power-hungry DF-MP2 keeping 20--32 bits is 4.9--8.3\pct{}
faster at 500--700\W; and wide-range, power-bound Gaussian-process geostatistics keeping 28 bits is 7.4--8.2\pct{}
faster at 500--700\W. At the default 1000\W{} cap HPL is still 3.9\pct{} faster and uses 7.5\pct{} less energy per
solve. Microbenchmarks give
upper bounds (FP64 GEMM keeping 27 bits: 6.6--21\pct{} at 300--1000\W; pure data movement: 18\pct{} less module power
at 1000\W), not application operating points. The gain comes mainly from fewer toggles in arithmetic units and data
paths, suiting FP64 solvers with residual correction or self-consistent iteration that feed multipliers directly
and in volume. Its limits are measurable: the crossover with INT8 emulation moves with shape and cap and
can be estimated from $(m,n,k)$ (8 of 8 held-out cases, all far from the crossover); with wide per-row exponent spans,
fixed-width row-sliced emulation can silently go wrong, while more slices or the automatic mode are correct but slower
than truncation; replacing every GEMM with FP32 or 3xTF32 fails on long inner products; kernels whose intermediates
regrow full mantissas gain little; in FP8 inference, further zeroing trades accuracy for speed. Zeroing costs one
bitwise operation per element, fusable into the producing kernel, needs no new hardware, and complements format
changes such as tile-level mixed precision. All HPL, HPL-MxP and MLPerf numbers here are research results, not
official scores.
