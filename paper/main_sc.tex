\begin{abstract}
When a GPU runs at its power limit, the energy of each operation sets the clock. Zeroing low-order mantissa bits is
known to lower GPU power in microbenchmarks; we ask whether doing it in software, on real data, is a usable optimization.
On an NVIDIA GH200 cluster and two 8-GPU AMD MI350X nodes, the saving comes from bit flips between neighbouring
operands: on MI350X, with values fixed, reordering alone changes the energy per FP64 operation by 8--9\pct, and flips
counted by direction predict held-out operand layouts ($R^2=0.93$). A first-order power model, calibrated by one locked-clock GEMM
on an application's own operands, predicts its speedups under caps within 1.2 percentage points on average. MI350X is
power-bound at its stock limit, so in-program truncation speeds up FP64 HPL, HPL-MxP, a Gaussian-process likelihood
and DF-MP2 by about 5--10\pct{} at default settings, each passing its accuracy check. Against INT8 emulation of FP64
the faster choice depends on the inner dimension, the cap and the tolerance; a per-call selector fitted on GEMM
measurements alone picks it in 98\pct{} of held-out GEMM cases on MI350X, 83--98\pct{} on GH200 and 22 of 24
Gaussian-process runs (always one method: at most 80\pct{} and 12 of 24). On data with wide per-row spans,
emulation can lose small entries while its normwise error stays small; a componentwise FP64 spot check detects the loss,
and one reference run per application decides whether it matters. Only truncation applies to BF16 and FP8.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Power is becoming the ceiling on delivered performance. Data centers cap rack power, competitions cap cluster power,
and accelerators ship with package limits that their own workloads reach: the AMD Instinct MI350X holds FP64, BF16 and
FP8 matrix multiplication at its default 1000\W{} limit 82--100\pct{} of the time in our measurements. Under a limit the
power manager lowers the clock until the device fits, so the speed of a program depends on how much energy each of its
operations consumes.

The effect we build on is known. DGEMM on random operands draws 67\pct{} more power than on constant ones on
A100~\cite{Bhalachandra2022}. Gregersen et al.\ showed that zeroing the least significant bits of GEMM inputs lowers
power, attributed it to fewer bit flips and proposed that compilers or schedulers exploit it~\cite{Gregersen2024}; a
zero-input DGEMM on GH200 draws 563 instead of 898\W~\cite{Antepara2025}; and an informal measurement showed that
predictable GEMM data also runs faster under a lower power limit~\cite{He2024}. Hardware designs narrow multipliers or
mask mantissas at unit inputs~\cite{Tong2000,Pool2011,Guenther2016,AMD2022}. What these works leave open is whether the
effect can be turned into a usable optimization: whether software truncation of \emph{real} operands speeds up
applications on production GPUs of different vendors while each application's accuracy check passes; whether the gain
under a cap can be predicted rather than swept; and when it is the better choice than a format change such as INT8
emulation of FP64~\cite{Ozaki2025,Uchino2025}, whose speed also depends on the power limit. We answer these questions on
two vendors' GPUs, ending in a selection we call \emph{switching-aware} because it accounts for the data-dependent
switching energy of each method's operands, which sets the clock under a cap:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Where the gain comes from and how much of it can be predicted.} Fills that stop the discarded bits toggling
without making them zero retain 65--97\pct{} (ones) and 41--92\pct{} (fixed pattern) of the saving in matrix engines,
FP64 vector units and memory copies of both vendors. With values and set bits held fixed, reordering operands alone
changes the energy per operation by 8--9 points, and bit flips between neighbouring operands, counted separately across
rows and along $k$, predict the energy of held-out layouts (leave-one-out $R^2=0.93$, against 0.79 for all flips and
0.34 for set bits; Section~\ref{sec:why}). If truncation only changes the switched capacitance, the truncated program
under cap $P$ behaves like the native program under $P_0+(P-P_0)/R$, with $P_0$ the measured idle power. Calibrated by
one locked-clock run per truncated variant (on GH200, which cannot lock clocks, one capped run), this first-order model
predicts 42 cap--format GEMM points per platform
with mean errors of 1.5 and 2.1 percentage points (at most 4.6 and 10.4). Calibrated from operand bit flips alone it predicts FP64 GEMM speedups within 1.2 points, a
test of only three predicted $R$ values, and overpredicts DF-MP2 by 4.4. Calibrated by a locked-clock GEMM on the
applications' own operands, weighted by its share of the dynamic energy, it predicts two applications not used to build
it within 1.2 points on average, as closely as a locked-clock run of the whole truncated program (0.8--1.7 over repeated runs) (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item \textbf{Gains that hold in real codes with their own accuracy checks.} On a GPU that is power-bound at its default
limit (MI350X), in-program truncation speeds up HPL, HPL-MxP (on its diagonally dominant test matrix and on a harder one),
DF-MP2 and a Gaussian-process likelihood by 4.6--10\pct,
replicated on a second node; on GH200 the same codes gain under caps and save energy at the default. Safe bit counts
must be validated per platform (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{When to choose INT8 emulation instead, per call.} At equal accuracy emulation is faster for long inner
dimensions at generous caps, truncation for short inner dimensions, tight caps and strict tolerances; limited memory and
wide per-row exponent spans also favor truncation, and of the two only truncation applies to BF16 and FP8. A per-call
selector fitted on GEMM measurements alone picks the faster method in 97.7\pct{} of held-out MI350X GEMM cases (always
emulating or always truncating: 52 and 48\pct) and, refitted on GH200, in 98.3 and 83.3\pct{} at tolerances of
$10^{-9}$ and $10^{-11}$ (always truncating: 60 and 80\pct). It matches DF-MP2, where emulation wins 99.3\pct{} of the
calls, and is right in 22 of 24 Gaussian-process runs whose block size moves the crossover with the cap, against 12 for
either fixed choice (Section~\ref{sec:format}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Input-dependent power.} Gregersen et al.\ varied value distribution, bit similarity and sparsity of GEMM inputs
on A100, H100 and V100 and saw power differ by almost 40\pct, but avoided throttling~\cite{Gregersen2024}; H.~He swept the
A100 power limit~\cite{He2024} and Antepara et al.\ measured GH200~\cite{Antepara2025,Antepara2025W}, both with all-zero
versus random inputs; Lucas and Juurlink modeled ALU power by the number of toggles~\cite{Lucas2016}. None truncates real
data, evaluates accuracy or applications, or compares vendors. For density fitting, Huang et al.\ adapt the precision of
INT8 emulation to the SCF state~\cite{Huang2026}; that changes the format, not the bits fed to FP64 units.

\textbf{Power-constrained HPC.} Manufacturing variation makes processors differ under the same power
bound~\cite{Rountree2012,Inadomi2015}; overprovisioning~\cite{Patki2013} and power-limit tuning~\cite{Zeus2023} decide
how power is distributed. We lower the energy each operation needs, so the two compose.

\textbf{Masking, precision tuning and data movement.} Mantissa masking in hardware saves energy in
multipliers~\cite{Tong2000}, shaders~\cite{Pool2008,Pool2011,Angerd2017}, baseband processors~\cite{Guenther2016} and
register files~\cite{Jahadi2024}; DRAM and HBM energy depend on toggling~\cite{Chatterjee2017,OConnor2017,Ghose2018}. Precision
tuning~\cite{RubioGonzalez2013}, mixed-precision storage~\cite{Higham2022,Grutzmacher2020} and transprecision
computing~\cite{Malossi2018} change types or bytes; zeroing changes neither. Low-precision LU with refinement underlies
HPL-MxP~\cite{Haidar2018,Dongarra2025}, and bit-information analysis can estimate needed bits from data~\cite{Klower2021}.

\textbf{FP64 emulation and format changes.} The Ozaki scheme splits a product into exactly computed low-precision
slices~\cite{Ozaki2012,Ootomo2024}; Ozaki scheme~II uses modular arithmetic~\cite{Ozaki2025} and is implemented in
GEMMul8 for CUDA and HIP~\cite{Uchino2025}; cuBLAS ships a fixed-point emulation with an automatic mode that picks the
bit count from the exponent span~\cite{cuBLASEmu,Schwarz2026}. Tile mixed precision converts whole tiles of geostatistics
factorizations to lower formats~\cite{Abdulah2019,Abdulah2022,Cao2022,Cao2023,Ren2025}, and convergence-driven schemes
switch formats or slice counts by solver state~\cite{Woo2023,Huang2026}. All of these change the format; truncation keeps
the original units, so the two can compose.

\section{Method and Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Truncation.} We keep the top $b$ of the $t$ fraction bits of an operand (52 for FP64, 7 for BF16, 3 for FP8
E4M3) and zero the rest, by truncation (a bit mask) or round-to-nearest (add half a unit of the last kept bit to the bit
pattern first, so ties round away from zero and a carry out of the fraction raises the exponent, which is the correctly
rounded result); the sign is untouched. All bit counts in this paper are kept fraction bits; for HPL, whose parameter counts significand bits, 35 fraction
bits correspond to its setting 36. HPL
rounds to nearest even; applications round to nearest; GEMM microbenchmarks and HPL-MxP truncate. For uniform operands the
relative error of $C=AB$ (normwise, $\|\hat C-C\|_F/\|C\|_F$, the error measure used throughout) is $\approx\alpha2^{-b}$ with $\alpha\approx0.27$ (nearest) or 0.70 (truncation), independent of
the inner dimension; truncation's error is a systematic shrinkage, which solvers with residual correction tolerate but
accumulating processes do not. On real data $\alpha$ ranges from 0.07 to 9.6 with cancellation, so tolerable bit counts
must be measured per application. With $p_n(P)$ and $p_r(P)$ the native and truncated throughput under cap $P$, the
equivalent saving $\Delta W(P)=p_n^{-1}(p_r(P))-P$ is the extra cap the native program needs to match the truncated one,
interpolated along the measured native curve. For plane-wave DFT we tie the bit count to the Davidson threshold
$\mathit{ethr}$: $b=\mathrm{clamp}(\lceil-\log_2\mathit{ethr}\rceil+m,16,52)$ with margin $m=0$.

\textbf{GH200.} Fourteen nodes with one GH200 480GB superchip each (Hopper GPU, maximum SM clock 1980\,MHz, FP64 tensor
peak 67\,TFLOP/s~\cite{NVIDIAGH200}), 200\,Gb/s InfiniBand, NVHPC 26.5 (CUDA 13.2). The module cap (GPU, CPU and memory)
is set from 200 to 1000\W; the default is also the maximum.

\textbf{MI350X.} Two nodes (n01, n02) with eight MI350X OAM GPUs each (gfx950, 288\,GiB HBM3E, maximum clock 2200\,MHz), ROCm 7.2.3,
Open MPI 5.0.7 with UCX. The per-GPU package limit is set from 1 to 1000\W; the default is the maximum; idle power is
244--275\W{} per GPU.

\textbf{Workloads.} GEMM microbenchmarks with cuBLAS or rocBLAS/hipBLASLt on operands uniform in $[-1,1)$
($m=n=32768$; inner dimension $k=2048$ for FP64, 6144 or 8192 for BF16 and FP8). HPL and HPL-MxP: on GH200 8 nodes (HPL tuned,
$N=286720$; HPL-MxP $N=491520$); on MI350X one node of 8 GPUs (HPL $N=540672$, $1\times8$; HPL-MxP $N=1034752$,
7.6\,PFLOP/s natively); HPL-MxP uses a diagonally dominant test matrix, which its rules allow for testing
only~\cite{HPLMxPRules}, and one run uses a matrix that is not (Section~\ref{sec:hpl}). Quantum ESPRESSO 7.6~\cite{Giannozzi2020} (Si\,1000 at 550\W, Au\,112 at 450\W; GH200).
DF-MP2 for taxol def2-TZVP (113 atoms): GPU4PySCF~\cite{Wu2025} on GH200; on MI350X, where GPU4PySCF does not run, the
same three-index tensor built by PySCF on the CPU and contracted on the GPU (energy within $\ee{2}{-13}$\Eh{} of PySCF and
$\ee{7.5}{-8}$\Eh{} of GPU4PySCF). An ExaGeoStat-like Gaussian-process likelihood on both ($n=80000$, Matérn-3/2,
blocked Cholesky with one FP64 DGEMM per panel). Truncation happens inside each program; GEMMul8~\cite{Uchino2025} and
cuBLAS emulation replace FP64 GEMMs through a hook or the math mode; cuBLAS emulation's bit count is its own
mantissa setting. GEMMul8 3.2.0 runs through its hook (HPL, DF-MP2 and GP cap sweeps, Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}), 3.5.4
through its direct API (shape sweep, selector, DF-MP2 per call, GP block sizes); both run DF-MP2 in 12.8--13.0\,s. Table~\ref{tab:where} lists where each code truncates.

\textbf{Measurement.} GH200: nvidia-smi samples every 250--500\,ms, and energy integrates 1-s averaged module power.
MI350X: a sampler reads all eight GPUs every 100\,ms, including a 15.26-$\mu$J hardware energy counter (energy is its
difference across a window) and the residency of power and thermal limiting. Every run records the cap in effect.
Variants run in ABBA order on the same nodes or GPUs after a cool-down; on MI350X all 8 GPUs run the same configuration
at once.

\textbf{A pitfall: fans and temperature.} On MI350X the chassis fans react to the previous run: the next run can be up
to 15\,\textdegree C cooler and 4.5\pct{} faster, because cooler silicon leaks less, and interleaving alone does not remove
this (it made 4 kept bits of HPL-MxP look faster than 3 at 800\W{} in one job). Regressing performance on the temperature
during a run would also remove the cooling that truncation itself causes; regressing on the temperature at its start
does not see the fan state (HPL runs start after a cool-down to a common temperature). We report raw paired ratios
(what a user obtains), start-adjusted values beside them, and controlled checks with a common thermal load before every
run: GEMM at steady state stays within 1.5 points of the short windows; HPL-MxP at 800\W{} restores the raw order (3 bits
$+10.4\pm2.8$, 4 bits $+6.0\pm3.5\pct$); DF-MP2 and the likelihood at 1000\W, with ABBA and BAAB rounds alternated,
give $12.6\pm0.1$ and $7.3\pm0.1\pct$ (20 and 28 bits) against 10.3 and 7.4 raw, 11.5--11.8 and 8.0--8.2 at equal start
and 13.3--14.2 and 7.1--7.3 at equal mean temperature. The spread between thermal methods (DF-MP2 10.3--14.2, the
likelihood 7.1--8.2\pct) exceeds every statistical interval we report, so we give raw values as the main result, which is
what a user obtains and, for DF-MP2, the most conservative. Fans can be read but not set with our access.

\textbf{Statistics.} Each speedup is a paired ratio on the same node, cap and ABBA round. Measurements that share a
chassis or a job are not independent, so on MI350X the unit is one measurement window of all 8 GPUs (GEMM), one node run
(HPL, HPL-MxP) or one round of all 8 GPUs (GP, DF-MP2), and every MI350X experiment is repeated on the second node. On
GH200 the units are node--pair samples from two node sets (HPL, HPL-MxP) or three nodes (GEMM), which treats pairs within
a job as independent; those intervals may be too narrow. Even on MI350X, units within one job may remain correlated,
so the intervals describe how stable a gain is on these nodes, not across a fleet; a mixed-effects model (random
intercept per block or round) gives the same estimates within 0.1 point and intervals of similar width.

\section{Why Fewer Mantissa Bits Save Power}\label{sec:why}

Dynamic power is roughly $\alpha_\mathrm{sw} C V^2 f$ with a data-dependent switching activity $\alpha_\mathrm{sw}$.
Under a cap the power manager lowers $f$ (and $V$) until the device fits, so a lower $\alpha_\mathrm{sw}$ becomes clock;
below the cap it appears as lower power. Both occur on both platforms. On GH200 at its default 1000\W{} module cap the
native FP64 GEMM is capped 95\pct{} of the time at 1822\,MHz; with 0 kept bits it runs at the maximum 1980\,MHz while
module power falls from 984 to 707\W. On MI350X at its default 1000\W{} package limit the native FP64 GEMM is held at the
limit 82\pct{} of the time at 1890\,MHz; with 0 kept bits it draws 785\W{} at 2183\,MHz and is 17\pct{} faster.

\textbf{Toggling or zero?} Zeroing stops the low bits from varying and makes them zero. Filling them with ones or a
fixed nonzero pattern instead also stops them toggling but is not zero. On GH200 we compare module power at 1000\W{} or
throughput at 500\W; on MI350X we lock the maximum graphics clock at 900, 1300 or 1700\,MHz, below the frequency any
configuration reaches, so that none is power-limited (PPT residency 0--0.6\pct) and the energy per operation compares
switching activity directly (8 GPUs $\times$ ABBA).

Most of the benefit survives such fills, in matrix engines, FP64 vector units and memory copies of both vendors
(Table~\ref{tab:fillmain}; all kernels in Supplement Table~S12), supporting the toggling hypothesis~\cite{Gregersen2024} beyond one vendor or unit. On MI350X the
shares barely change with the clock (FP64, 27 bits: 81--83\pct{} at 900--1700\,MHz), while the total saving grows with it
(FP64, 0 bits: 20, 24 and 29\pct{} less energy per operation), as expected when $CV^2f$ grows relative to static power.
The effect is not limited to matrix engines: an FP64 multiply-add loop with 0 kept bits draws 553 instead
of 862\W{} on GH200 at 1000\W{} and runs 33\pct{} faster on MI350X at 600\W, and a memory copy, power-bound on MI350X at
600\W, runs 28\pct{} faster.

\textbf{Bit changes, not values.} Fills also change values. Three further controls on MI350X keep values or set bits
fixed and vary only how bits change between the operands that meet in the matrix engine (Table~\ref{tab:flips}).
Placing the same values per row and column in sorted instead of shuffled order along $k$ lowers the energy per operation
by 8--9 points with 52 kept bits and by 8--9 points with 0, with identical values and set bits. Random dropped bits that
differ between rows but repeat along $k$ (krep) save almost nothing, whereas the transpose, random along $k$ but shared by
all rows (mrep), saves about half as much as zeros: the engine sees operands from neighbouring rows as well as
neighbouring $k$ in succession, and not with equal weight. We fit the energy change $\Delta E$ (\%) of the 15 layouts on
the changes from native of the flips per neighbouring pair along $k$ ($\Delta F_k$: consecutive $k$ in a row of $A$ or
column of $B$) and across rows ($\Delta F_r$: neighbouring rows of $A$, columns of $B$), each the mean over $A$ and $B$
of per-bit-position counts of the measured operands, and score each model by predicting every layout from the other 14
(leave-one-layout-out $R^2$, three runs). The total number of flips gives 0.79 and set bits 0.34.
$\Delta E=a\,\Delta F_k+b\,\Delta F_r+c\,\Delta F_k\Delta F_r$ without intercept gives 0.93 (largest error 5.8 points), with
$a=0.062$, $b=0.43$, $c=-0.021$ at 1300\,MHz on n01: near native a flip across rows costs seven times one along $k$, and
the negative interaction makes a bit save most when it is quiet in both directions,
which is why krep and mrep do not add up to zeros. Weighting flips by bit significance fails ($R^2<0$), and
separating sign and exponent from mantissa bits adds nothing. The asymmetry suggests that the operand path sees row
neighbours more often than $k$ neighbours, as when one operand is broadcast across lanes; we cannot observe the dataflow.
These controls cover FP64 on MI350X only.

\textbf{A second clock effect on MI350X.} With the clock locked and nothing power-limited, the effective clock still
depends on the data: at a 1300\,MHz lock the native FP64 GEMM runs steadily at 1171--1179\,MHz (5th--95th percentile, 8
GPUs) and with 0 kept bits at 1235--1240\,MHz; native BF16 at 1088--1100\,MHz and with 0 bits at 1132--1142\,MHz, at
59--63\,\textdegree C, while power and thermal residency and the per-XCD ``clock below host limit'' counters read zero.
Circuit-level clock stretching or droop protection would explain it; we have no direct measurement. Truncation relieves
it too.

\section{A Model of Truncation Gains Under a Power Cap}\label{sec:model}

Let a GPU under cap $P$ settle at a target clock $f$. Its voltage $V(f)$ is set by the target clock, but switching
happens at the effective clock $sf$, where $s\le1$ is the data-dependent clock reduction of Section~\ref{sec:why}
($s=1$ when there is none). With $c$ the program's effective switched capacitance and $P_0$ the power that does not scale
with switching,
\begin{equation}
P = P_0 + c\,s\,V(f)^2 f = P_0 + c\,s\,g(f),
\end{equation}
and performance is proportional to $sf$. Truncation changes $c$ and $s$, not $g$. For the native ($n$) and truncated ($r$)
program define
\begin{equation}
R=\frac{c_rs_r}{c_ns_n},\qquad \sigma=\frac{s_r}{s_n};
\end{equation}
at a common locked target clock, $R$ is the ratio of their dynamic powers and $\sigma$ the ratio of their effective
clocks, both directly measurable. Under cap $P$ the truncated program settles where $c_rs_rg(f_r)=P-P_0$; the native
program reaches the same target clock $f_r$ under $P'$ with $c_ns_ng(f_r)=P'-P_0$. Dividing,
\begin{gather}
P'=P_0+\frac{P-P_0}{R},\qquad p_r(P)=\sigma\,p_n(P'),\label{eq:pred}\\
\Delta W_f=P'-P=(P-P_0)\left(\tfrac{1}{R}-1\right),\label{eq:dwf}
\end{gather}
where $p_r(P)=\sigma\,p_n(P')$ because both run at target clock $f_r$ and performance scales with $s$. $\Delta W_f$ is
the extra cap the native program needs to reach the truncated program's \emph{target clock}. The equivalent saving used
in our results, the extra cap it needs to reach the truncated program's \emph{throughput}, is
\begin{equation}
\Delta W=p_n^{-1}\!\bigl(\sigma\,p_n(P')\bigr)-P,
\end{equation}
which equals $\Delta W_f$ only when $\sigma=1$ (GH200, by assumption) and exceeds it when $\sigma>1$ (MI350X,
$\sigma=1.01$--1.05). Every $\Delta W$ reported in this paper is measured, by interpolation along the measured native
curve; the model is used only to predict speedups. The model assumes
that $s$ does not change with $f$ over the range used and that the program stays compute-bound; predictions are capped
at the program's clock-limited peak. The model needs the native curve $p_n(P)$ and $P_0$; it saves measuring every
truncated variant under every cap. We set $P_0$ to the measured idle power, independent of the validation data: 174\W{}
for a GH200 module (three nodes 169--179\W) and 262\W{} per MI350X GPU. The truncated kernel is characterized by
locked-clock runs at 1300\,MHz (MI350X; $R$, $\sigma$; no point of the cap sweep) or by its speed at the lowest cap
$P_\mathrm{min}=300$\W{} (GH200, which we could not clock-lock; $\sigma=1$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:pred} inverts to
$R=(P_\mathrm{min}-P_0)/(p_n^{-1}(p_r(P_\mathrm{min}))-P_0)$; that point is excluded from evaluation), and predictions are
compared with the GEMM sweeps at the other caps (400--1000\W{}, seven caps per format on both platforms).

Fig.~\ref{fig:model}). Fitting $P_0$ on all other formats and predicting the held-out one gives 1.0 and 2.3; treating all

For the FP64 widths applications keep (27--36 bits) the error is at most 2.9 points; it is largest where the truncated
clock lies beyond the native curve. \textbf{Baselines.} Carrying the speedup measured at one cap to the others errs by
19 and 10 points on average (GH200, MI350X; largest 73 and 48), the locked-clock energy ratio or clock ratio alone by 6.4 and
15.9 respectively (MI350X), and linear interpolation between the lowest and highest cap by 6.0 and 1.8, against 1.7 and 2.0 for the
model on the same points: on MI350X the model is not more accurate, but it needs no capped run, interpolation two. A
fitted $P_0$ (190--250\W) lies below the idle power, absorbing the shape error of $g(f)$, so we use the idle value. \textbf{From bit flips.} $R$ and $\sigma$ can
also be predicted without running the truncated variant: fitting them, at the 1300\,MHz lock, on the flip changes of
the 12 layouts that are not plain truncation (Supplement Table~S7) and evaluating the fit on the flips of truncated
operands gives $R=0.935$, 0.877 and 0.619 for 36, 27 and 0 kept bits (measured 0.934, 0.887, 0.654), and the resulting

they test only three independent predictions of $R$ and $\sigma$, all from synthetic operands. For a whole application this GEMM-derived fit is not enough: applied to the flips of DF-MP2's
tensor it predicts $R=0.90$ and 0.81 for 32 and 20 bits (measured 0.92, 0.86) and overpredicts the gains by 4.4 points on
average (largest 9.0). The cause is the kernel, not the data: at DF-MP2's shape ($2002\times302302\times5477$, $A$
transposed) synthetic operands give $R=0.915$ and 0.848, within 0.005 of DF-MP2's own operands, so the per-flip energies of
the fit belong to the kernel it was fitted on and must be refitted per shape. \textbf{External validation.} The GEMM points were used to develop the model. As a test on programs that played no part
in it, we ran DF-MP2 and the Gaussian-process likelihood at the 1300\,MHz lock on node n02, derived $R$ and $\sigma$ from
their whole-program energy and time ($R=0.92$ and 0.86, $\sigma=1.02$ and 1.03 for DF-MP2 with 32 and 20 bits; $R=0.94$
and 0.91, $\sigma=1.01$ and 1.02 for the likelihood with 36 and 28 bits), and predicted their speedups at 500--1000\W{}
from their native curves on the same node. Over the 16 points the mean error is 0.8 percentage points and the largest
2.9. The sampler and the energy counter read inside the program agree within 0.001 in $R$ here, as on the GEMM (0.0007).

(errors 1.1 and 2.3; 1.1 and 1.7 over all 16 points): in one, its unchanged non-GEMM phases drew 3--7\pct{} less power in

(DF-MP2, 32 and 20 bits) and 0.927 and 0.886 (likelihood, 36 and 28). With $\varphi$ the GEMM's share of the native
dynamic energy and the other phases unchanged, $R_\mathrm{app}=1-\varphi(1-R)$ and $\sigma_\mathrm{app}=1+\varphi(\sigma-1)$,
a first-order form that treats the run as one phase. In the likelihood, read at phase boundaries, the other phases
(POTRF, TRSM, covariance, solve) draw 363 against 650\W, so $\varphi=0.97$ although the GEMM takes 90\pct{} of the time,
and the GEMM's in-program $R$ (0.885--0.907, 0.930--0.940 in two runs) brackets the panel's; in DF-MP2 the GEMM takes
96\pct{} of the time and 95\pct{} of the dynamic energy. This predicts the 16 speedups (four $R$ values at four caps)
within 1.2 points on average (largest 3.4; without $\varphi$, 1.5), within the 0.8--1.7 of the whole-program runs. A truncated program's curve thus follows from one locked-clock GEMM on its
operands to within a few points. Errors of 1--2 points are small against gains of 5--20 points, not against the margins near a crossover.

\subsection{Quantum ESPRESSO: Tolerance-Driven Bit Counts (GH200)}\label{sec:adaptive}



Plane-wave DFT tolerates 20--28 kept bits; Gaussian-basis DFT needs 32--36 (Supplement~S1). Tying the bit count to the
Davidson threshold, in-program, reproduces the native total energy in every printed digit ($10^{-8}$\Ry) on both systems
with the same SCF iteration count, while cutting time by 2.1--3.6\pct{} and energy by 2.2--3.4\pct{} against the unmodified
program (Supplement, Table~S6); $b$ rises from 16 to 39--42 as the SCF converges. On Si\,1000 the work is unchanged
(177--178 Davidson iterations), so its gain is a power effect. On the gold surface fixed 24 bits keep the native 138
Davidson iterations and shorten the SCF by 3.2\pct, the power effect at equal work; the tolerance-driven rule needs 126
(9\pct{} fewer, for reasons not yet identified) and shortens it by 4.5\pct, so its extra gain comes from the iteration
count. The rule needs no safe bit count in advance and matches or beats the best fixed count.

\section{The Boundary with INT8 Emulation of FP64}\label{sec:format}



Emulation wins by a wide margin where its fixed costs amortize at a generous cap (long inner dimensions: HPL, DF-MP2,
GEMM with $k\ge2048$); a tighter cap or accuracy threshold shrinks or reverses its lead, and it loses where the inner
dimension is short (the likelihood, GEMM with $k=1024$). Truncation gains less but in every case we measured, needs no
extra memory, and alone applies to BF16 and FP8.

\subsection{Crossover: Shape, Cap and Memory}\label{sec:crossover}

Emulation trades FP64 arithmetic for several times as many integer operations, so its benefit grows with the ratio of
INT8 to FP64 throughput, about 30 on GH200 and about twice that on MI350X. On GH200, cuBLAS emulation at the accuracy of
35-bit truncation ($\approx10^{-11}$) is 11--28\pct{} slower than truncation on a square GEMM with $k=2048$, and wins only
for long inner dimensions; across 480 measurements a three-parameter model in $1/m+1/n$ and $1/k$ describes GEMMul8 with
$R^2=0.98$--0.99 (Supplement~S4). On MI350X the crossover lies further toward emulation, and the cap moves it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}). Truncation to 35 bits ($\ee{7.8}{-12}$) is 5--6\pct{} faster than native at 1000\W{} and
8--9\pct{} at 500\W{} for every $k$ from 1024 to 8192, whereas GEMMul8 with 12 moduli ($\approx\ee{5}{-13}$) goes from
$-13$ to $+121\pct$ at 1000\W{} and from $-45$ to $+48\pct$ at 500\W. Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} compares the two at equal
accuracy, sweeping the kept bits (20--44) and the moduli (8--14). With $k=4096$ emulation is faster at every accuracy
except the tightest at 500\W; with $k=2048$ it is faster at every accuracy at 1000\W{} but at 500\W{} only for errors
of $10^{-8}$ or more. With $k=1024$ the order depends on the accuracy and the cap: at 1000\W{} emulation wins
for errors above about $10^{-10}$ (8 moduli: +27\pct{} at $\ee{1.3}{-8}$, against +10\pct{} for 24-bit truncation) and
truncation below (+4.8 against $-6.2\pct$ near $\ee{5}{-12}$); at 500\W{} truncation wins at every accuracy. Emulation
draws the full limit, so under a cap its extra integer work is paid for with clock, whereas truncation removes work from
the power budget; on GH200 the same holds for GEMMul8, while cuBLAS emulation, which draws only about 706\W{} at
1000\W, gains ground at lower caps: the direction depends on the emulation's own power.

\textbf{Costs that appear only in applications.} Emulation needs workspace: at our best native HPL configuration
($N=540672$) the matrix fills 272 of each GPU's 288\,GiB and GEMMul8's workspace no longer fits, so HPL must give up
problem size first. Through the BLAS hook each new GEMM shape costs a one-time setup (DF-MP2's first pass over its 226
shapes: 138--141\,s instead of 13\,s, in GEMMul8 3.2.0 and 3.5.4 alike; the times above exclude it), which calling the
library directly with one workspace removes. In the likelihood at $n=80000$ the hooked DGEMM of the first trailing update
($m=n=78976$, $k=1024$) returned success but an invalid result in all 256 runs: GEMMul8 batches its moduli's INT8
products with output stride $mn$, and ROCm 7.2.3's strided-batched INT8 GEMM silently leaves every batch after the first
unwritten once the stride reaches $2^{32}$ elements, independent of the data, a deployment risk of replacing a BLAS call
wholesale.

\subsection{Exponent Span: When Emulation Silently Fails}\label{sec:span}

Truncation keeps every element's exponent; Ozaki-type emulation slices each row relative to its maximum, so elements far
below the maximum lose bits. On data with a wide per-row span this can go wrong silently (Supplement Table~S14): in
Gaussian-basis DFT on GH200, where 24--44\pct{} of the flop-weighted nonzeros lie more than $2^{-32}$ below their row
maximum, fixed 36-bit cuBLAS emulation lets the SCF converge normally to a total energy 52.5\,mE$_\mathrm{h}$ off, and
GEMMul8 diverges, whereas rounding to 36 bits errs by $\ee{9.6}{-7}$\Eh. With 40 bits or the automatic mode the emulation is
correct but 1.5--2.1$\times$ slower than truncation or native. Uniform synthetic spreads over up to 64 (MI350X) or 256
(GH200) binades barely affect GEMMul8 (MI350X, 12 moduli: p99 error at most $\ee{4.4}{-11}$), so a wide per-row span,
which an interposer can measure beforehand (Supplement Fig.~S1), is necessary but not sufficient for this failure;
narrow-span codes (HPL, Quantum ESPRESSO, DF-MP2) do not suffer it. It does not flag defects such as the size
overflow of Section~\ref{sec:crossover}; a check of the result does. Replacing every FP64 GEMM with FP32 or
3xTF32 fails for a different reason, accumulation over long inner products (Quantum ESPRESSO, Supplement~S3).

\textbf{A post-hoc spot check} tests the result instead: after emulating, recompute 64 random rows (or columns, along
the larger dimension) of $C$ natively and compare normwise. It catches gross errors: at the size failure ($m=n=65536$)
the error is 32 and the estimate 31.6, and on a synthetic pair whose product terms all lie $2^{-40}$ below the row
maxima the estimate matches the full error (100, 25 and 0.1\pct{} with 10, 12, 14 moduli). It misses the wide-span loss:
on the exchange--correlation operands of the valinomycin DFT, rebuilt on the CPU (DF-B3LYP/def2-TZVP, energy within
$10^{-10}$\Eh{} of GH200's), GEMMul8's normwise error ($10^{-10}$ to $10^{-12}$) is that of 28--36-bit truncation, but its
componentwise error $\max|\Delta C|/(|A||B|)$ reaches 1, with 19--62\pct{} of the entries off by more than $10^{-6}$
(36-bit truncation: none above $10^{-9}$). Computed on the same 64 rows (one more DGEMM, on $|A|$ and $|B|$, ignoring entries whose bound $|A||B|$ underflows below
$10^{-290}$), the componentwise error detects it: 1.0 on the DFT exchange--correlation operands against $10^{-11}$ for truncation. GP panels lose
entries the same way (componentwise 1.0) without any effect on the likelihood, so the product alone cannot tell harmful
from harmless loss, and a converging SCF (Supplement Table~S14) is no check either. The componentwise check costs
0.6--0.7\pct{} of the emulated call on the DF-MP2 and GP shapes but more than the call on the small DFT GEMMs
($m=n\le2958$), so it should run on the first calls of each shape, not every call.




\subsection{Choosing per Call}\label{sec:selector}

The pattern above can be turned into a per-call rule. For a GEMM call $(m,n,k)$ under cap $P$ with a tolerated relative
error $\varepsilon$ of the product, each method takes its cheapest setting whose measured error is at most
$\varepsilon$ (kept bits, or moduli), and the method with the smallest predicted time wins. Truncation's speedup barely
depends on the shape (35 bits: 4.6--6.8, 5.0--7.4 and 8.0--9.8\pct{} over 22 shapes at 1000, 650 and 500\W), so it is
one number per cap, or the model of Section~\ref{sec:model}. Emulation's time relative to native is modelled as
$r_e = c_0 + c_1/k + c_2\,(1/m+1/n)$ per cap and number of moduli, fitted on 22 shapes ($m=n$ from 4096 to 32768, $k$ from 512
to 8192, two rectangular; GEMMul8 3.5.4, $R^2=0.97$--0.99); calls through the hook add its one-time setup per shape. The
rule uses only GEMM measurements. Emulated calls are spot-checked componentwise (threshold $10^{-6}$) on the first
calls of each shape; a failed check repeats the call natively and, if the error is a wide-span loss rather than a gross
error, admits emulation for that application and input only after one reference run, native against emulated, meets the
application's tolerance. On GH200 that run rejects valinomycin (52.5\,mE$_\mathrm{h}$); on MI350X it admits the likelihood
($|\Delta\ell|\le\ee{4.2}{-7}$). A wide per-row span (more than 1\pct{} of the elements more than $2^{-32}$ below their row
maximum) predicts which inputs need it.

discriminate: emulation wins 99.3\pct{} of its calls, as the rule predicts. The likelihood does, because its block size
sets $k$ (1024 to 4096) and the crossover moves with the cap, from $k=1536$ at 1000\W{} to 4096 at 500\W{} ($k=2048$:
$+21.7$ against $+4.1\pct$ at 1000\W, $-19.8$ against $+8.3\pct$ at 500\W). The rule is right in 22 of 24 runs, missing
two within 4\pct{} of the crossover; either fixed choice is right in 12, the best $k$ threshold (in-sample) in 18. These 22 assume that the likelihood's reference
run admitted emulation; refusing emulation on the span alone would cost up to 32\pct{} there ($k=4096$, 1000\W). \textbf{From an application tolerance.} The rule's tolerance is that of the product. For the likelihood a factor
$\kappa=|\Delta\ell|/\varepsilon$, the larger of two earlier truncated runs ($\ee{4.1}{6}$), maps a tolerance $\tau$ on
$|\Delta\ell|$ to $\varepsilon=\tau/\kappa$. For $\tau=10^{-2}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-6}$ the rule takes 28, 36 and 40 bits; a
separate run ($n=64000$, block 2048, 1000 and 500\W) confirms its choice in all six cases, but 40 bits gives
$\ee{1.2}{-6}$ because $\kappa$ varies fourfold between runs, so the mapping needs a margin of a bit or two. Emulation
needs its own $\kappa$ ($\ee{5.2}{5}$, ten times smaller): with it the rule takes 9, 11 and 12 moduli, all within $\tau$,
without changing its choices. $\kappa$ need not come from full runs: it grows roughly with $n$
($\kappa/n=36$--77 in runs of a few seconds at $n=16000$ and 32000, 25--78 at 64000--80000), and $77n$ would have taken
44 instead of 40 bits at $10^{-6}$.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Where truncation pays.} Three conditions decide the gain: the GPU must be power-bound, the truncated operands
must flow directly and in volume into multipliers or data paths, and the algorithm must tolerate the error, ideally by
correcting it. The model makes the first quantitative; the second explains why GEMM-dominated solvers gain 4--20\pct{} and
codes whose intermediates regrow full mantissas (MFEM, SW4, LAMMPS) little; the third separates solvers from AI
weights (Supplement S7). On GH200 the gain at the default cap is mainly energy; on MI350X, whose default limit binds for FP64, BF16
and FP8 matrix work, truncation turns into 4.6--10\pct{} more speed at stock settings; a part not power-bound at stock settings should see energy instead (not measured). Truncation
is not an alternative to clock tuning: at a fixed clock it lowers the energy per operation (FP64 GEMM, 36 and 27
kept bits: 4.8 and 8.1--8.4\pct{} at 1300\,MHz), so it lowers the cost of whatever operating point a cap or a frequency
choice selects.

\textbf{Truncation and emulation.} Emulation is the stronger lever where it can amortize its fixed costs (long inner
dimensions, generous caps, narrow exponent spans, ample memory, few distinct shapes); truncation elsewhere, and only
truncation applies outside matrix multiplication and to BF16 and FP8. Building the selector into a BLAS dispatcher, with
the componentwise spot check and per-input reference runs, remains to be done.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~Two platforms, one generation each, and at most two nodes (16 GPUs) on MI350X; other parts manage
power and cool differently. (ii)~Safe bit counts depend on platform and problem size. (iii)~HPL-MxP was run on the diagonally dominant test matrix and
one non-dominant generator of ours, not an official one. (iv)~Each scientific code was tested on one or two inputs; on MI350X DF-MP2 uses our own contraction of
PySCF's tensor. (v)~Analysis units within one or two nodes may be correlated within a job; on GH200 node sets differed more than
pairs within a set. (vi)~ABBA cancels linear thermal drift but not fan feedback; temperature-adjusted values were checked
under controlled thermal history for GEMM, HPL-MxP, DF-MP2 and the likelihood, and fan speed could not be fixed. (vii)~Default caps are also the highest; the limiters' time scales are unknown.
(viii)~Power comes from the devices' sensors and accumulators, not an external meter; GH200 module power includes the
Grace CPU. (ix)~The MI350X clock reduction attributed to clock stretching is an inference. (x)~Part of the Quantum ESPRESSO gain on the gold surface comes from 9\pct{} fewer Davidson iterations, for unidentified reasons. (xi)~GEMMul8's
memory limits and the size failure are observations about one software stack (ROCm 7.2.3), and GEMMul8 was not run for
DF-MP2 on GH200. (xii)~Each emulation safeguard was validated on one or two cases. Checking only the first calls
of a shape assumes the operands' span does not grow during the run (SCF densities, late Cholesky panels); re-checking
when the span statistic changes would remove it. A reference run pays off only for inputs evaluated many times, such
as a likelihood inside a maximum-likelihood loop. (xiii)~TOP500 requires full
FP64 arithmetic~\cite{TOP500Linpack} and HPL-MxP allows diagonally dominant matrices for testing only~\cite{HPLMxPRules}:
our HPL and HPL-MxP numbers are research results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On GPUs of two vendors, truncating unused mantissa bits in software buys performance for HPC applications under power
limits while their results stay within each code's own criterion. The saving comes from bits that change between
neighbouring operands; a first-order model predicts an application's performance--cap curve from one locked-clock GEMM
on its own operands, and bit flips predict the saving for the GEMM shape they were fitted on. MI350X is power-bound at its stock limit, so truncation speeds up HPL, HPL-MxP (on a diagonally dominant and a harder test
matrix), a Gaussian-process likelihood and DF-MP2 by 4.6--10\pct{} at default settings and 7--19\pct{} at 500\W. INT8
emulation is faster for long inner dimensions at generous caps, truncation for short inner dimensions, tight caps and
strict tolerances; a per-call selector fitted on GEMM measurements chooses between them on both platforms. A componentwise
spot check detects gross emulation errors and wide-span losses alike; whether a loss matters takes one native-versus-emulated
reference run per application and input, since a converging SCF can still be 52.5\,mE$_\mathrm{h}$ off.
Truncation also applies to BF16 and FP8; safe bit counts must be validated per platform and size. All HPL and
HPL-MxP numbers are research results, not official scores.
